# Standalone Mode

This file covers the rules and patterns for decks the user is *not* there to deliver — leave-behinds, exec summaries, async distribution, pre-reads. Read this when the mode chosen in Phase 1 is "standalone" or "both".

The premise: Winston designed his framework for a speaker carrying the content. When no speaker is present, the slide has to do that work. Winston's *structure* (promise → vision → proof → contributions, no thank-you slide) is preserved because it's about the audience's journey, not about who is speaking. His *design rules* (≤15 words, no titles, no logos) relax because the constraints they were solving for — preserving the speaker as the main event — no longer apply.

This is not a betrayal of Winston. It's a deliberate adaptation. A Winston-pure slide read async fails because nothing is carrying it. A standalone slide delivered live fails because the audience reads instead of listens. Both failures are real; the right answer is to know which mode you're in.

---

## The headline rules for standalone mode

| Rule | Standalone | (Delivery for comparison) |
|------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Words per slide | ~75–100 (target); 150 ceiling | ≤15 target, 25 cap |
| Font size, body text | 24pt minimum | 40pt preferred |
| Font size, footnotes | 18pt minimum | 20pt floor |
| Slide titles | **Required** on body slides | Omitted by default |
| Page numbers | **Required** | Not used |
| Logos | Allowed in master | None |
| Background patterns | Subtle, brand-appropriate OK | None |
| Bullets | Allowed where parallel | Sparingly |
| Charts | Full legend + axis labels + source | ≤2 series, minimal labels |
| Context/background lines | **Required** under each major idea | Carried verbally |
| Exec summary | **Required** at front | Not used |
| Glossary slide | Allowed when jargon present | Not used |
| Q&A / contact slide | Allowed at end (after Contributions) | Never |
| Citations | Inline or footnote | Not used |

## The standalone deck skeleton

The full structure, slide by slide:

### Slide 1 — Title slide

Title, author/owner, date, version number. Standalone leave-behinds need provenance because they get forwarded, archived, and re-shared. A reader six months later needs to know what they're reading and whether it's current.

```
[Title of the talk]

[Author/Owner Name]
[Date]
v[X]
```

### Slide 2 — Executive summary

This is the most important slide in a standalone deck. Many readers will spend more time on this slide than all the others combined; some will only read this slide. ~75–100 words covering:

- **Situation**: what's true today (1 sentence)
- **Complication**: what's changing or at risk (1 sentence)
- **Key findings**: the 2–3 things the reader most needs to know (sentence each)
- **Recommendation**: what should happen next (1 sentence)

Treat this as the empowerment promise made good in a single page. The reader who stops here should still walk away with the gist.

### Slide 3 — Vision

Title: a noun phrase naming the problem (e.g. "Concentration Risk in Q3 Growth"). Body: ~75–100 words covering the problem someone cares about, what's new in the approach, and why this matters now. Citations as needed.

### Slide 4 — Proof of done-something

Title: a noun phrase naming the work (e.g. "What We Did"). Body: the concrete steps already taken. A small chart or timeline often works well here. Charts get full legends, axis labels, and source citations.

### Slides 5 through N — Body

Each major idea gets its own titled slide. The pattern for each body slide:

1. **Title** (3–7 words, content-bearing): names the idea
2. **Headline statement** (1 sentence at top, larger font): the take-away
3. **Body** (~50–75 words): the substance — argument, data, analysis
4. **Context/background line** (1–2 sentences in smaller font, bottom or sidebar): the ambient context an async reader needs but a live audience would have absorbed verbally

This 4-part anatomy is the standalone equivalent of Winston's "speaker says it, slide handles it." The headline statement is the speaker's voice. The body is what they would have elaborated. The context line is what the speaker would have established earlier in the room.

### Optional — Glossary / definitions slide

If the deck uses jargon, acronyms, or technical terms a reader might not know, include a glossary slide before the contributions. Title: "Definitions" or "Glossary". Body: term + 1-line definition for each, alphabetical.

### Optional — Hapax legomenon

The "one deliberately overwhelming slide per deck" rule still applies in standalone mode. If used, pair it with a caption explaining why it's there ("This slide is intentionally complex — the point is the complexity itself"). Async readers don't have a speaker to deliver Winston's verbal punchline.

### Slide N+1 — Contributions

Same as delivery mode. Title: "Contributions". Body: the specific, concrete things the work added. Mirrors the executive summary's recommendations.

This is non-negotiable across modes — never "Conclusions", "Summary", "Thank you", "Questions?", or a URL.

### Optional last slide — Q&A / Contact

Standalone mode allows a conventional touch the delivery mode forbids: a slide with anticipated questions and brief answers, plus contact info for follow-up. Title: "Anticipated Questions" or "For Follow-Up". Body: 3–5 likely questions with 1-sentence answers, then contact information.

This slide comes *after* the Contributions slide, never replacing it. The Contributions slide is still the rhetorical close; the Q&A slide is operational.

## Translating the Big Four for standalone

Winston's Big Four — cycle, fence, verbal punctuation, rhetorical questions — were designed for a speaker. In a standalone deck, each translates into a visual or structural pattern. **Decide per slide what each idea needs**; not every slide needs every translation.

### Cycling

In delivery, the speaker says the same idea three times in different framings to catch the foggy 20% of the audience. In standalone:

- **Visual cycling**: the executive summary states the headline, the body slides develop it, the contributions slide restates it. The reader encounters the same idea three times in different forms — summary, evidence, takeaway.
- **Repeated motifs**: a small symbol or color cue that reappears across slides anchored to the same idea. The reader's eye learns to track it.
- **Headline echoes**: each body slide's headline statement should feel like a variation on the executive summary, not a non-sequitur.

### Fence (near miss)

In delivery, the speaker draws a verbal boundary around the idea by contrasting it with neighboring ideas. In standalone:

- **A "What this is / What this isn't" callout** on slides where the idea is in near-miss territory. Two columns, three or four lines each, sharp contrast.
- **Inline contrast sentences**: "Note: this is X, not Y. The difference is Z."
- **Comparison tables**: when the idea sits in a crowded conceptual field, a small comparison table earns its space.

### Verbal punctuation

In delivery, the speaker says "we just finished X, now we turn to Y" so the foggy 20% can rejoin. In standalone:

- **Section divider slides**: a slide with just a section number and label (e.g. "2 of 3: The Risk") between major sections. Clean, generous whitespace, high contrast.
- **Running header showing section**: the slide title bar can include a section indicator like "Part 2: Risk Analysis · Slide 7 of 12".
- **A table of contents slide** at the start of body content: lists the sections and their starting slide numbers. The reader's ToC is the standalone equivalent of the speaker's verbal landmarks.

### Rhetorical questions

In delivery, the speaker poses a question and pauses seven seconds. In standalone:

- **A question as a slide title**: "Why is product line A's growth a risk?" The body answers. The reader's eye follows the same arc the audience's ear would have.
- **A pull-quote callout** highlighting the question, with the answer below or on the next slide.
- **Sparingly**: standalone readers can't be made to wait seven seconds, so the technique loses some of its force. Use once or twice per deck, not throughout.

The decision per slide is: which of these does this idea actually need? A purely informational slide may need none. A pivotal idea may need cycling + fence. A counterintuitive finding earns the rhetorical question. Match technique to need; don't apply all four mechanically to every slide.

## The 4-part body slide anatomy in detail

Every body slide in standalone mode follows this structure. The ratios are approximate; the order is fixed.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Title (3–7 words, content-bearing)               │ ← What is this slide about?
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                  │
│ Headline statement.                              │ ← The take-away in one sentence.
│ One sentence, larger font.                       │   This is the speaker's voice.
│                                                  │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                  │
│ Body content — ~50–75 words.                     │ ← The substance.
│ Argument, data, analysis. May include            │   Bullets allowed where parallel.
│ chart, table, or diagram in place of text.       │   Charts get full legends.
│                                                  │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Context: 1–2 sentences in smaller font.          │ ← Ambient context for async reader.
│ Source: [citation]                               │   Source citation if data shown.
│                                                  Page N
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The headline statement is the most important element. If the reader skims and reads only the title and headline of every slide, they should still get the argument. This is sometimes called the "skim test" — if the deck fails it, the headlines are doing too little work.

## Reader notes (standalone-mode speaker notes)

In standalone mode, the speaker notes pane gets reused for **reader notes**. The deck is the only output, so these notes become a value-add for the async reader rather than dead weight.

For each slide, write 3–6 sentences covering:

- **Annotations**: what the reader should focus on, why this slide matters in the larger argument
- **Anticipated questions**: "A natural question here is [Q]. The answer is [A]." This substitutes for the live Q&A the user won't be there to handle.
- **Further reading or sources**: where the reader can dig deeper
- **Cross-references**: "See slide 7 for the data behind this claim." or "This connects back to the risk identified on slide 4."

Reader notes do *not* narrate what's on the slide — the slide already does that. They add value the slide can't carry.

In `.pptx` output, reader notes go in the standard notes pane. In HTML output, they go in a collapsible block under each section. In Markdown output, they appear under each slide as a `**Reader notes:**` block.

## On charts and data visualization

In delivery mode, Winston counsels two-series-max with minimal labels because the speaker explains. In standalone, the chart has to explain itself.

**Required for every chart**:
- Title (above) — what the chart shows in plain language
- Axis labels with units
- Legend (if more than one series)
- Source citation (below or at right) — "Source: [name], [year]"
- A 1-sentence takeaway in the slide's headline statement — what should the reader notice?

**Allowed in standalone that wouldn't be in delivery**:
- Up to 4–5 series if genuinely needed
- Annotations directly on the chart pointing to specific data points ("Q3 spike: product line A launch")
- Tables of underlying data alongside the chart, in smaller font, for the reader who wants to verify

**Still avoid even in standalone**:
- 3D effects on charts (always)
- Pie charts with more than 5 slices
- Tables with more than ~10 rows on a single slide (split or move to appendix)

## On the executive summary slide specifically

This slide deserves disproportionate care because it's the most-read slide in the deck. The pattern that works:

```
Executive Summary

Situation:     [1 sentence — what's true today]

Complication:  [1 sentence — what's changing or at risk]

Findings:      • [Finding 1, with quantification if available]
               • [Finding 2]
               • [Finding 3]

Recommendation: [1 sentence — what should happen next, who owns it]
```

The four-part SCQ-then-recommendation structure lets the reader scan in 30 seconds. Keep each section short. If the recommendation requires elaboration, that's what the body slides are for — don't bloat the exec summary trying to make it self-sufficient. It's a doorway, not a destination.

## Common standalone failure modes

When writing a standalone deck, watch for these pitfalls:

**The "deck of speeches"**: every slide is a paragraph of prose. Standalone allows more words than delivery, but a wall of text is still a wall of text. Use bullets, callouts, charts, and tables to chunk the content visually.

**The "live deck pasted into standalone"**: minimalist Winston-style slides with only "Product line A: 47% of growth, 12% of customers" and no context. The async reader has no idea what this means. This is the most common failure when the user is converting an existing delivery deck — they leave the slides untouched and just remove themselves from the equation.

**The "everything in the appendix"**: stuffing all the substance into appendix slides while keeping body slides skeletal. The body slides are where the argument lives. Appendix is for raw data and reference material, not load-bearing content.

**The "missing exec summary"**: skipping straight from title to vision. Async readers need the one-page synthesis up front; delivery audiences don't because the speaker provides it verbally.

**The "thank you slide returns"**: the convention is so strong that "thank you" or "questions?" slides creep back in standalone mode. They're still wrong. The Contributions slide is still the close. The Q&A slide is operational and follows Contributions; it doesn't replace it.

## When to recommend Both mode

Suggest Both mode (delivery in front + standalone appendix in back) when:

- The user will deliver live AND distribute the deck afterward
- The deck will be archived or referenced after the meeting
- The audience includes people who couldn't attend live
- Stakeholders may want to forward parts of the deck to others

Suggest Standalone-only when:

- The deck is a leave-behind, pre-read, or async distribution from the start
- The user is a writer of the deck but not a presenter (e.g. preparing material for someone else to send out)
- The deck is a one-pager or briefing that no one is presenting

Suggest Delivery-only when:

- The deck will only ever be presented live and won't be shared
- The user explicitly wants Winston-pure minimalism
- Adding a standalone appendix would dilute the live impact

When in doubt, ask the user how the deck will be distributed after delivery. Their answer points to the right mode.
