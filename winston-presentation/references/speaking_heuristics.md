# Speaking Heuristics

This file expands the structural and verbal heuristics that the main SKILL.md gestures at. Read this when you reach Phase 5 (speaker notes) or whenever you need to draft the verbal performance, not just the slides.

---

## The Big Four

Winston identifies four heuristics he uses in every talk. Cycle, fence, verbal punctuation, rhetorical questions. Apply them in the speaker notes, not on the slides themselves.

### 1. Cycle

**Principle**: at any moment, roughly 20% of the audience is fogged out. Saying something once means a fifth of the room missed it. Saying it three times — in different framings — gets the message through.

**The three passes Winston recommends**:
1. **Brief**: load the schema. "We need to talk about why product line A is at risk."
2. **Detailed**: fill in the schema. "Here's the customer concentration data, here's the competitor positioning, here's the regulatory exposure."
3. **Indexable summary**: tell the audience what's worth remembering. "So: product line A's risk comes from three places — concentration, competition, regulation. If you take one thing away from this section, it's that."

This is not "tell them what you'll tell them, tell them, tell them what you told them" delivered mechanically. Each pass approaches the idea from a different angle.

### 2. Fence (a.k.a. near miss)

**Principle**: people understand a new idea by contrasting it with neighboring ideas they already know. If you don't draw the boundary, the audience will assume your idea is the same as something they're familiar with — and they'll be wrong.

**Use it when**: introducing any concept that could be confused with a similar one.

**Verbal pattern**: "This may sound like [familiar idea], but the difference is [X]. With [familiar idea], you get [Y]; with this, you get [Z]."

**Winston's example**: explaining what an arch *is* by also showing what an arch *is not* — a stack of two blocks side by side, a stack with a gap. The fence makes the boundary visible.

### 3. Verbal punctuation

**Principle**: the foggy 20% needs landmarks where they can rejoin the talk. Without explicit markers, they stay lost.

**Verbal pattern**: at the boundary between sections, name what you just finished and what's coming next.
- "We just finished talking about [section A]. Now we turn to [section B]."
- "That was the *what*. Now I want to talk about the *why*."
- "So those were the three risks. Let me move to the recommendations."

**On slides**: a single horizontal rule, a section divider slide, or a numbered milestone (e.g. "3 of 5") functions as visual punctuation that reinforces the verbal cue.

### 4. Rhetorical questions

**Principle**: asking the audience a question — even one they don't answer aloud — re-engages the language processor. Done right, it pulls drifting attention back.

**The rules**:
- **Not too easy** — if the answer is obvious, the audience feels patronized and won't speak.
- **Not too hard** — if no one knows the answer, the question dies.
- **Wait the full seven seconds** — the silence feels like an eternity but it's the standard. Don't fill it.

**Use sparingly**. One or two rhetorical questions per talk is enough. More than that and they lose force.

## Opening rules

### Do not start with a joke

The audience is still putting laptops away, settling in, and adjusting to the speaker's vocal parameters. A joke at the start usually falls flat. Save jokes for later in the talk, when the audience knows you.

### Do start with the empowerment promise

The first words out of the speaker's mouth (and the first slide) should answer this question: *what will the audience know at the end of this that they don't know now?*

**Verbal pattern**: "By the end of the next [duration], you will know [X]. And [X] will [concrete benefit]."

The promise has to be specific. "You'll learn about our Q3 results" is not a promise — it's a topic statement. "You'll know which two of our six product lines drove 80% of growth, and why one of them is at risk" is a promise.

### Inspire by exhibiting passion

Winston says people are inspired by speakers who exhibit visible passion about what they're doing. Inspiration is not separate from content; it is content delivered with the speaker's genuine investment audible in their voice. In speaker notes, flag the moments where the speaker should let their genuine excitement show.

## The 5-minute rule (job talks and persuasive talks)

For any talk where the speaker is being evaluated — a job talk, a thesis defense, a pitch, a board presentation — the audience must see two things within the first five minutes:

1. **Vision**: a problem someone cares about, plus what is new in the speaker's approach
2. **Proof of done-something**: concrete steps already taken, not vague claims of accomplishment

If both aren't established within five minutes, Winston says the speaker has already lost. The room has decided.

This rule shapes the deck structure: the first ~25% of slides need to deliver vision and proof. Nothing decorative belongs in that opening stretch.

### Situating

Adjacent to the vision/proof rule is *situating*: place the work in time, space, and field.

- **Time**: what's the trajectory of this area? What came before? Why does this moment matter?
- **Space**: who else is working on this problem? Where are they? How does the speaker's work relate?
- **Field**: what are the consequences of this result for the broader field?

A talk that fails to situate leaves the audience unable to assess significance. Even a great result feels small if it's presented in a vacuum.

## Winston's Star (the 5 S's)

For talks where the speaker wants the ideas to travel beyond the room — to be remembered, retold, cited — Winston offers a five-element checklist. Most strong talks have at least three of the five. The strongest have all five.

### Symbol

A visual handle for the idea. Something that can be drawn on a napkin. Winston's example: an arch, used as the symbol for one-shot learning. The arch is concrete, simple, and memorable; it gives the idea a physical anchor.

**Question to ask the speaker**: "If someone wanted to draw your idea on a napkin, what would they draw?"

### Slogan

A one-sentence linguistic handle. Not a tagline, not a marketing phrase — a sentence the audience can repeat verbatim when someone asks them what the talk was about.

**Question to ask the speaker**: "When someone in the audience tells a colleague tomorrow, in one sentence, what your talk was about — what do you want them to say?"

### Surprise

Something counterintuitive. The element that makes the audience say "wait, really?" Without surprise, the idea feels obvious — even if it isn't.

**Question to ask the speaker**: "What part of your work, when you first realized it, surprised even you?"

### Salient idea

One idea that sticks out above all others. Not the most important idea necessarily — the one that's most visibly different, the one with edges.

**Question to ask the speaker**: "If the audience remembers exactly one thing from your talk a month from now, what is it?"

### Story

The narrative of how the work came to be. The obstacles, the dead ends, the moment it clicked. Stories ground abstract ideas in concrete experience and give the audience something to retell.

**Question to ask the speaker**: "What's the story of how this work came together? What was the moment it clicked?"

## Closing rules

### Don't end with "Conclusions"

The conclusions slide is forgettable. Replace it with a **Contributions** slide that lists, specifically and concretely, what the speaker added. The contributions slide mirrors the empowerment promise. Promise made; promise kept.

### Don't end with "Questions?"

The contributions slide should be the slide that's up during Q&A. A "Questions?" slide is wasted real estate at the most-viewed moment of the entire talk.

### Don't end with "Thank you"

Winston is firm on this. "Thank you" implies the audience stayed out of politeness — that they did the speaker a favor by listening. The right close is a **salute**, not a thank-you.

**Verbal pattern for the salute**: "You've been a great audience" / "It's been a pleasure presenting to this group" / "I've enjoyed my time at [venue]." A compliment without subordination.

### A joke is fine at the end

By this point the audience knows the speaker. Humor lands. If the speaker has a clean joke that fits the topic, the close is the right place for it.

### Call for questions, but stay in control

Aim for a conversation, not an interrogation. Repeat each question back so the rest of the audience can hear it. Be prepared to ask yourself the first question if no one volunteers.

## On the underlying mechanics

Two things to remember when drafting speaker notes:

**One language processor**. Humans cannot read a text-heavy slide and listen to a speaker at the same time. They will choose one. They will choose the slide. So if there's text on the slide, the speaker's words must be *different* from the slide's words. Otherwise the audience reads ahead and tunes out.

**Empathetic mirroring**. Winston believed that audiences feel something physically when a speaker writes on a board or handles a prop — mirror neurons fire as if the audience member were doing the work themselves. Slides don't trigger this. So when a deck has to do the lifting that a board would do in a teaching context, the speaker has to compensate verbally — with rhythm, with pauses, with gesture.
