# Teaching Notes — coaching a first-timer through each section

The user is learning. For each section below you'll find: the *why* (explain a version of this in your own words, briefly), the *questions* that draw out a good answer, and the *traps* to watch for. Teach in small doses tied to the section at hand — never dump all of this at once.

---

## Customer

**Why it matters:** Everything downstream depends on a sharply defined customer. "For everyone" produces a product for no one. A precise segment lets you judge whether the problem is real, whether the market is big enough, and whether the solution fits.

**Ask:**
- Who, specifically, is this for? Picture one real person — their role, situation, constraints.
- What's different about this segment versus adjacent ones you're *not* targeting?
- Is this the person with the problem, the person who pays, and the person who uses it — or are those different people?

**Traps:** "Everyone," "all businesses," "any consumer." Gently push back and ask them to narrow until the segment feels concrete. Use the classic example: a new car for "urban single professionals under 35 in apartments" is a very different product from one for "suburban families of five who carpool to soccer."

---

## Problem

**Why it matters:** The problem must be real, important to the customer, and big enough that people will pay to solve it. A great product aimed at a problem nobody cares about goes nowhere (Amazon's Fire Phone is the cautionary tale — impressive tech, no real customer problem).

**Ask:**
- In the customer's own words, what's the pain?
- What do they do about it today? (There's always *something* — even if it's a workaround or doing nothing.)
- Why does today's approach fall short?
- How many people have this problem, and would they pay to fix it?

**Traps:** Describing the solution instead of the problem. Stating a problem with no real market. Assuming the problem rather than grounding it in something you've observed. Mark assumptions explicitly as things to validate.

---

## Solution

**Why it matters:** The solution has to address *that* problem — directly and simply — and beat the current alternative on better / faster / cheaper. A solution that drifts from the stated problem, or that just matches existing options, means going back to the drawing board.

**Ask:**
- In plain terms, what is the product and how does it solve the problem you just named?
- Line it up against the problem: does it solve *that*, or has it wandered into a different (maybe more exciting) problem?
- How is it meaningfully better/faster/cheaper than what they use today?
- Does it require solving at least one genuinely hard problem? (If not, it may not be differentiated enough — that's a useful signal, not a failure.)

**Traps:** Feature laundry lists. Solving a different problem than the one stated. Claiming differentiation without saying on which dimension. "Skills-forward" solutions shaped by what's easy to build rather than what the customer needs.

---

## Headline, sub-headline, summary, quotes, CTA

**Why it matters:** These translate the thinking into something a customer would actually find compelling. If you can't write an exciting, honest one-liner, the idea underneath may be weak. Writing them last (after customer/problem/solution) makes them much easier.

**Ask:**
- If this launched tomorrow and a customer read the headline, would they get it and want it?
- What would a real customer in your segment actually say about the benefit? (Make the quote sound human.)
- Why does *your team* care about building this? (Spokesperson quote.)

**Traps:** Jargon, hype, vagueness. Quotes that sound like a press office. A launch date that's a meaningless placeholder — pick a date you'd actually aim for.

---

## FAQ (general coaching)

**Why it matters:** The press release is the destination; the FAQ is how you prove you can get there. This is where honesty lives. The strongest sections are usually "what has to be true for this to succeed" and "the top three reasons this will fail." A draft with no failure modes isn't strong — it's incomplete.

**Coach the mindset:** You're truth-seeking, not selling. It's good to surface problems you can't yet solve — write them down as open questions. Reviewers are there to help improve the idea, not to applaud it. The goal of the eventual review meeting is a clear-eyed go/no-go, reached by debate, not a standing ovation.

**On TAM specifically:** Don't let a number get asserted. Walk through it: how many customers have the problem × how much each would pay. If they can't estimate it, that itself is an open question worth flagging.

---

## How the document gets used (worth telling the user once)

- First draft: a few hours, solo. Expect holes.
- Then: share early and often with a few cross-functional peers; revise.
- Review meeting: circulated and **read silently** for 15-20 minutes, then 40 minutes of questions and debate. The author takes notes and revises.
- Multiple drafts and reviews follow for promising ideas.
- Ends in a go / no-go decision. Many good ideas get killed here — cheaply — and that's the system working (funnel, not tunnel).
