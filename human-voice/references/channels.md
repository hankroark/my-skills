# Channel Guide

Each channel has its own AI tells and its own idea of what sounds natural. Read the section that matches the job. The core rules in SKILL.md still apply everywhere.

## Contents
1. Email
2. Chat (Slack, Teams, texts)

## 1. Email

**What a good email does:** the reader knows why you wrote within two sentences, knows exactly what you need, and can reply in under a minute.

**Rules**
- Lead with the point or the ask. Context comes after, and only what the reader doesn't already know.
- One ask per email. If there are two, number them, or send two emails.
- Make the ask specific and easy to say yes to: "Can you approve the $4,200 PO by Thursday?" not "Let me know your thoughts when you get a chance."
- Subject line names the actual topic: "PO approval needed by Thu: test fixture rework", not "Quick question" or "Following up".
- Match the greeting to the relationship. A first name or nothing for close colleagues; "Hi Sam," for most; formal only where formality is real.
- One sign-off. "Thanks," or the user's usual. Not "Thanks so much for your time and consideration. Warm regards, Best,".
- Most AI emails are two to three times too long. Cut until removing anything would lose meaning.

**Email-specific tells**
- Greetings: "I hope this email finds you well", "I trust this message finds you in good spirits", "Hope you had a great weekend" (to a stranger).
- Openers: "I wanted to reach out because", "I'm writing to inform you that", "Per our previous conversation", "As per my last email", "Just wanted to quickly check in".
- Closers: "Please don't hesitate to reach out", "I look forward to hearing from you", "Thank you for your time and consideration", "Warmest regards".
- Fake personalization: "I was impressed by your recent work in the industry." If you can't name the specific thing, delete the flattery.
- Politeness stacking: three thank-yous and two apologies in one email. One thanks is enough.
- A "PS" that contains the real request.
- Template feel: the email could be sent to anyone by changing the name.

**Difficult emails** (pushback, bad news, saying no): state the news or the position in the first or second sentence. Give the reason in plain terms. Say what happens next. Warmth comes from being clear and respectful, not from padding.

## 2. Chat (Slack, Teams, texts)

**What good chat looks like:** how the person actually types to coworkers. Short, direct, ask first.

**Rules**
- No greeting-body-signoff structure. Just say it.
- Lead with the ask or the action item, then one or two lines of context.
- Four or five sentences is the ceiling. Longer than that, put the TL;DR first or move it to an email or a doc.
- Sharing a link: one line on why it matters, not a summary of it.
- Match the channel's tone. Lowercase and fragments are fine if that's how the team writes.

**Chat-specific tells**
- Over-formal phrasing: "I wanted to reach out regarding", "Please be advised", "At your earliest convenience".
- Corporate filler: "Just wanted to flag", "Wanted to surface this", "Looping in @name for visibility".
- Apologetic hedging: "Sorry to bother you, but", "Not sure if this is the right channel, but", "I might be wrong, but".
- Emoji used to soften every sentence or manufacture enthusiasm.
- Bulleted, bolded, headed messages that read like a memo.

## 3. Social

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn has the densest concentration of AI and ghostwriter templates of any channel, so readers there are primed to spot them. The posts that do well over time give people something specific enough to save, disagree with, or act on.

**Rules**
- Open with the specific thing: what happened, the number, the decision, the claim you'll defend. Not a lesson, a proverb, or a teaser.
- Write real paragraphs of two to four sentences. One-sentence-per-line formatting is the most recognizable ghostwriter pattern on the platform.
- Include at least one thing worth saving: a named tool, a concrete step, a number with context, a decision you made and why.
- Include something specific enough that a smart person could disagree. Generic takeaways get "So true!" and nothing else.
- Short posts under about 1,300 characters; long ones under 3,000.
- Zero to three hashtags, worked in naturally or left off.
- End on a real final thought or a genuine question you want answered. No engagement bait.
- If sharing a link, consider putting it in the first comment instead of the post body; many practitioners report off-platform links reduce reach.

**LinkedIn-specific tells**
- Engagement bait: "Agree?", "Thoughts?", "Drop a 🔥 if...", "Repost if this resonated", "Comment YES and I'll send you...".
- Information-withheld hook: "At my last company we had one unspoken rule. It changed everything." State the rule.
- Curiosity templates: "What if I told you...", "Here's what nobody tells you about...", "Most people get this wrong."
- Common-belief-then-counter: "X is for beginners. That's what most people think. They're wrong."
- Cliché proverb opener: "Work smarter, not harder."
- Credential stacking before advice: "I've hired 200 people. Built 3 teams. Scaled to $50M."
- Vulnerability bait: a failure story told mainly to hook, ending in a tidy lesson.
- Achievement formula: emotion word plus announcement, thanks to the team, a universal lesson, an emoji-closed "Excited for what's next! 🚀".
- Fake dialogue: "CEO: Why do we...? CMO: Because..." used to launder an opinion as a conversation.
- ALL-CAPS single words: "We onboarded HUNDREDS of customers."
- Period-separated emphasis: "Every. Single. Day."
- Arrow chains: "Problem → Insight → Action → Result".
- Tag-and-thank lists of five or more people.
- A formal self-introduction paragraph at the bottom.

### X / Threads

- One idea per post. Cut every word that isn't working.
- Threads: each post should stand alone and still make sense if quoted. No "1/🧵" hype opener; just start with the first point.
- No hashtag stacks. No "Let that sink in."

### Instagram captions, YouTube community posts, video descriptions

- First line carries the hook because it's what shows before "more". Make it concrete.
- Write like you'd caption a photo for a friend who knows the subject: the specific detail, what was hard, what you'd do differently.
- For gear or technique posts, the specifics are the value: settings, model names, what went wrong.
- Video descriptions: one or two plain sentences on what the video covers and who it's for, then timestamps or links. No "In this video, we'll dive deep into..."

### Bios

- Say what you do and for whom, in plain words. One specific detail beats three adjectives.
- Avoid: "passionate", "driven", "results-oriented", "helping X do Y at scale", "on a mission to".

## 4. Business documents

Memos, reports, proposals, briefs, decision docs, executive summaries.

**Rules**
- Put the conclusion or recommendation first. Busy readers read the first paragraph and decide whether to keep going.
- Every section answers a question the reader actually has. If you can't name the question, cut the section.
- Use numbers with units, dates, owners, and costs. "Reduce cycle time" means nothing; "cut review cycle from 9 days to 4 by moving sign-off to the program lead" means something.
- State tradeoffs and risks plainly, including the ones against your recommendation. A document with no downside reads as a sales pitch.
- Headings are fine in longer documents. Write them as plain statements ("Cost is driven by two vendors"), not title-case labels ("Key Cost Drivers").
- Bullets for genuinely parallel items: options, requirements, action items. Prose for reasoning, because reasoning needs "because" and "so".
- Executive summaries: three to five sentences. What's the situation, what do you recommend, what does it cost, what do you need from the reader.

**Business-specific tells**
- "Challenges and Opportunities" and "Future Outlook" sections that say nothing.
- "This document aims to provide a comprehensive overview of..."
- Recommendations hedged into mush: "It may be worth considering potentially exploring..."
- Benefits listed as abstract nouns: "efficiency, scalability, and alignment".
- Every risk "mitigated" with no mechanism described.

## 5. Procedures and technical writing

Use ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English principles for instructions, procedures, work instructions, technical documentation, and anything a reader will act on step by step. STE was built for maintenance manuals, where ambiguity costs money or safety.

**Rules**
- One instruction per sentence. Write instructions in the imperative: "Torque the bolt to 25 Nm."
- Short sentences: about 20 words maximum for procedures, 25 for descriptive text.
- Name the actor and use active verbs. "The system sends an alert," not "An alert is sent."
- One term for one thing, every time. Never vary a term to avoid repetition.
- Use words with one clear meaning. Avoid idioms, phrasal verbs with several meanings ("set up", "carry out"), and figurative language.
- Keep noun clusters to three words or fewer. "Pressure relief valve inspection procedure" becomes "the procedure to inspect the pressure relief valve".
- Put conditions before actions: "If the light is red, stop the pump."
- Put warnings and cautions before the step they apply to, not after.
- State the expected result where it helps: "The indicator turns green."
- Keep code, commands, part numbers, identifiers, product names, legal text, and required quotations exactly as given. Never simplify them.

Don't claim formal STE conformance; that requires checking the current ASD-STE100 issue and its controlled dictionary. Apply the principles and flag any term that needs a domain exception.
