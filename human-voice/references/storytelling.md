## 1. What gives AI stories away

StoryScope (Russell et al., 2026) compared about 10,000 human-written stories against stories five AI models wrote from the same prompts. It found AI fiction is detectable from narrative choices alone, with style stripped out entirely, and that editing out clichés and purple prose barely changed this. The giveaways are structural. Scrubbing words won't fix them; you have to make different story decisions.

**AI over-explains its theme.** Narrators stated the story's meaning outright in about 77% of AI stories versus 52% of human ones. A grief story ends with the narrator spelling out what the character learned. Dialogue turns into philosophical debate.
- Fix: never state the lesson. End on an action, image, or line of dialogue and let the audience draw the conclusion.

**AI tells stories in a straight line.** Tight cause and effect, one plot thread, events in chronological order, everything resolved. Most AI stories had no subplot at all.
- Fix: start somewhere other than the beginning (the aftermath, the moment it went wrong). Use a flashback or a jump forward. Let a second thread run alongside. Leave something unresolved.

**AI resolves through acceptance and tidy agency.** The protagonist reaches internal understanding, makes the right choice, and the ending is clean.
- Fix: let outside events matter. Let the main character be partly wrong, or right for the wrong reasons. Moral ambiguity was far more common in human stories.

**AI over-writes bodies and senses.** It showed emotion through physical sensation in 81% of stories versus 38% for humans: tightening chests, cold sweat, a breath they didn't know they were holding. It leaned on smell and on weather or lighting that mirrors mood. Humans just named the feeling about three times as often.
- Fix: sometimes write "She was scared." Use one precise physical detail when it does real work, not three stacked ones. Let setting be setting.

**AI avoids the real world.** It made vague allusions instead of naming real books, songs, brands, places, or people. Humans named specifics about twice as often.
- Fix: name things. The actual camera, the actual street, the actual song on the radio, the actual manager's name (with permission).

**AI writes as if no one is listening.** Humans addressed the reader directly and broke the fourth wall far more often.
- Fix: in narration especially, talk to the viewer. "You've probably had this happen." "I know how this sounds."

**AI stories all land in the same place.** The five models clustered together in narrative space; human stories were rarer and more varied.
- Fix: before writing, list the obvious version of the story, then change at least two structural choices (where it starts, who's right, how it ends).

## 2. Claude's own fingerprint

StoryScope found Claude had the most distinctive narrative profile of the AI models tested. Since this skill runs on Claude, counter these deliberately:

- **Flat escalation.** Claude's events escalated less than any other source. Stakes stay level and nothing boils over. Fix: make each beat raise the cost, risk, or pressure. Let something actually go wrong.
- **Uniform narrative voice.** One even, measured tone throughout. Fix: let the voice shift with the moment. Faster and blunter when things go wrong, looser when they're funny.
- **Reverence for convention.** Claude honors storytelling traditions instead of bending them. Fix: break a convention on purpose: open at the end, skip the setup, cut a scene off early.
- **Quiet endings and epilogues.** Claude favors a calm wind-down and a tidy epilogue over an abrupt or dramatic ending. Fix: end closer to the climax. Cut the epilogue. A hard stop is often stronger.

Other models have their own habits (GPT leans on gossip and decades-later reflection; Gemini on bleak settings and long tidy denouements), which matters if the user asks you to fix text another tool produced.

## 3. Story moves that read as human

- **Start late.** Begin as close to the turning point as you can. Cut the warm-up.
- **One specific moment beats a summary of many.** "The third time the gimbal died, I was on a ledge with the sun dropping" beats "Over the years I've had many equipment failures."
- **Show the wrong turn.** What you tried first that didn't work is often the most interesting and most trusted part.
- **Use real dialogue.** People interrupt, trail off, and talk past each other. They don't speak in complete, balanced sentences.
- **Let the facts carry emotion.** A detail the audience can feel ("I'd driven four hours for this shot") does more than telling them how you felt.
- **Leave a loose end.** Not every question needs an answer. An honest "I still don't know if that was the right call" is memorable.
- **Escalate.** Each beat should cost more, risk more, or reveal more than the last.

## 4. Writing for the ear

Scripts are heard once, at the speaker's pace. The reader can't skim back.

- **Read every line out loud.** If you stumble, rewrite it. This is the single most useful check.
- **Shorter sentences than in print,** but not all short. Vary them the way people talk.
- **Contractions always.** "It's", "don't", "you'll". Uncontracted speech sounds robotic.
- **Front-load the subject.** "The battery died" not "What happened next, after I'd set everything up, was that the battery died."
- **Repeat key terms.** On paper, repetition is clumsy. On audio, it helps people keep up.
- **Signpost lightly.** "Here's the problem." "So that's the setup." Natural spoken transitions are fine, unlike their written AI cousins, because a listener needs orientation. Keep them short and don't stack them.
- **Avoid tongue-twisters and number clusters.** "Fifteen fifty-two point four" is hard to hear. Round or show it on screen.
- **Write for the speaker's mouth.** If a voice profile exists, match the user's spoken habits, not just their written ones.

## 5. YouTube script structure

Treat these as defaults to bend, not a template to fill. The StoryScope lesson applies: identical structure on every video becomes its own tell.

**Hook (first 10 to 30 seconds).** Tell the viewer, concretely, what this video gives them or show the most interesting moment first. A specific result, a surprising image, a problem they recognize.
- Avoid: "Hey guys, welcome back to the channel", "In this video, we're going to dive deep into", "Have you ever wondered...?" stacked questions, and clickbait the video doesn't pay off.
- Good shape: "I shot this entire wedding on a camera that costs less than the lens I usually bring. Here's what broke, and what surprised me."

**Setup (brief).** Only the context needed to follow along. Who you are comes later, if at all, and in one line.

**Body.** Organize around turns, not topics. Each section should change something: a problem appears, an attempt fails, a discovery lands. Put a small payoff every minute or two so the viewer has a reason to stay. Show the specifics: settings, part numbers, what you'd actually click.

**Re-hooks.** Before a slower section, give a reason to keep watching that is specific and honest: "The fix ended up being a five-dollar part, but first I tried three expensive things."

**Ending.** Deliver the payoff, then stop. One concrete next step or a genuine question for the comments is fine. Skip the recap, the "so that's it for today", and the long subscribe plea. If you need a call to action, make it one line and specific.

## 6. Script format

Default to a two-column feel even in plain text: what's said, and what's shown. Unless the user has their own format, use:

```
[VISUAL: close-up of the dead battery indicator]
VO: Forty minutes into the shoot, the battery died. The spare was in the car.

[ON CAMERA]
So here's what I should have done.
```

- Mark B-roll, on-screen text, and graphics in brackets so the spoken lines stay clean.
- Put numbers that are hard to say into on-screen text instead.
- Keep each spoken block to what one breath or one shot can carry.
- If the user wants a teleprompter version, strip the bracketed directions.

---

## 7. Final check for scripts and stories

1. Does anything state the theme or lesson outright? Cut it.
2. Does it start at the beginning and run in a straight line? Consider starting later or out of order.
3. Do the stakes rise, or stay flat?
4. Is the ending a quiet wind-down or epilogue? Try ending earlier.
5. Are emotions shown only through bodies and weather? Name one plainly.
6. Are real things named (gear, places, people, songs)?
7. Does the narration ever talk directly to the viewer?
8. Read aloud: any line you'd stumble on?
9. Is every story detail real, or marked as a placeholder for the user to fill? Never invent personal experiences for the user.
